1# Raises the number to the power of `b` as `number`. A zero exponent
2# gives one. An integer exponent is expanded by exponentiation-by-squaring
3# with every product carried in two doubles, `wide` being the power so far:
4# `halved` splits a factor by Dekker's method, as `sine` does, so that
5# `joined` keeps what each product drops, and the answer is rounded once.
6# A factor above about 1.3e300 overflows when `halved` multiplies it by
7# 134217729. Such a factor is divided by two to the 28th and split there,
8# and the high half is multiplied back: subtracting it from the factor
9# restores the low half, so the split stays exact and the pair is kept.
10# `10.power 305` is then the double nearest to the true power.
11# A negative exponent takes the `reciprocal` of the pair. Where the `answer`
12# overflows, comes to nan or to zero, the squaring is done in single doubles.
13# A fractional exponent of a positive base is taken as e raised to `b` times
14# the natural logarithm of the base. Zeros, infinities and nan follow the
15# same IEEE rules that Math.pow obeys.
16#
17# `expo` says that `b` is not a number itself, rather than letting the
18# first atom it reaches say it of its own attribute: the exponent of a
19# string is eight bytes short, and the comparison that met it blamed its
20# own receiver, which is an attribute the caller never wrote (#9283).
