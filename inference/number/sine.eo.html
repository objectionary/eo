1# Sine of `num` radians as `number`.
2#
3# The angle is reduced twice: whole turns come off it first, which leaves
4# it in [-pi, pi], and then quarter turns, which leaves it in
5# [-pi/4, pi/4]. The answer is the sine or the cosine of what is left, with
6# the sign the quarter asks for. One reduction is not enough: an angle near
7# a multiple of pi lands at the edge of [-pi, pi], where the Taylor series
8# of the sine cancels its leading digits and keeps almost none of them, so
9# `pi.sine` came back as `6.98e-16` against the `1.22e-16` it is, and
10# `355.sine` was two hundred thousand of its last places out (#9279).
11#
12# What is reduced is the magnitude, and the sign of the argument is put
13# back on the answer, so that the sine of a negative angle is exactly the
14# negation of the sine of its magnitude. Reducing the argument itself is
15# not odd, because `floor` is not: `pi` lost a whole turn where `-pi` lost
16# none, and the two were summed at opposite ends of the range.
17#
18# The reduction takes `turns` whole turns off the magnitude, and the
19# accuracy of the answer is the accuracy of that one subtraction: at `1e15`
20# the reduced angle is what survives of a number sixteen digits long, so a
21# product of `turns` and two pi rounded into a single double keeps none of
22# the digits that decide the sine. The product is therefore carried in two
23# doubles.
24#
25# `tau` is the nearest double to two pi, and `2.4492935982947064e-16` is
26# what that double misses. `turns times tau` is taken exactly by Dekker's
27# method: `134217729` is two to the 27th plus one, and multiplying by it
28# and subtracting back splits a double into halves narrow enough that their
29# pairwise products round nowhere: `turns` into `top` and `foot`, `tau`
30# into `crown` and `stub`. The four partial products of those halves then
31# say what the single rounded `product` dropped, and `slip` is that
32# dropped part.
33#
34# What the whole turns leave is carried in two doubles as well, rather than
35# folded into one. `coarse` is the magnitude less the rounded product,
36# which is exact, since the two stand a few units apart; `fine` is
37# everything the correction still owes it. `angle` is their difference and
38# is read for one thing only, to say which quarter the angle is in, as
39# `steps`. The quarter turn itself comes off `coarse`, before `fine` is
40# paid: that subtraction is exact too, by Sterbenz, so `rest` keeps every
41# digit of an angle near a multiple of pi/2, while a single rounded `angle`
42# would have left it with the width of its own last place, which is all of
43# a sine of `1e-5`. `quarter` is a quarter of `tau`, and what `tau` misses
44# is divided by four the same way, both exact, since dividing by four only
45# moves an exponent.
46#
47# `sined` is the Taylor series of the sine over `rest` and `cosined` that
48# of the cosine, both summed through Horner's scheme, and `picked` reads
49# one of the two off `steps`: the sine in the quarter around zero, the
50# cosine in the one above it, and the negation of either on the far side,
51# since a sine shifted by two quarters is the negation of itself.
52#
53# Two doubles carry about 106 bits, so the reduction holds to a few ULP for
54# as long as `turns times two pi` needs no more than that, which is |num| up
55# to `1e15`. Past it the reduced angle drifts out of [-pi, pi] and the series
56# is summed so far from zero that it answers outside [-1, 1], which is no
57# sine of anything: `1e300` came back as infinity. A finite angle above
58# `1e15` therefore terminates, rather than answering a number a sine cannot
59# be. Holding the whole range of a double would take Payne-Hanek reduction,
60# with two-over-pi written out to more bits than any product of doubles
61# carries. Non-finite arguments collapse to nan, as they did.
